The cupping room at the Addis export warehouse is not romantic. Strip lights, a
concrete floor, twenty-four bowls on a rotating table, and a fan that does not
work. We had flown in on the Tuesday and by Thursday morning we had tasted
sixty-one coffees, which is roughly forty more than anyone should taste in a
week if they still want opinions at the end of it.

Table four was Guji. Six lots, all naturals, all from washing stations within
about thirty kilometres of each other. Five of them were what you expect from a
Guji natural in a good year: blueberry, a bit of booze, a sweetness that arrives
late and stays. Good coffees. Ones you could put on filter and nobody would
complain.

The sixth was not like the others.

## The argument

Rami — who had come along on the grounds that he cooks the food and therefore
has a vote — scored it an 88. I gave it an 84 and put a line through it.

His note said *jasmine, peach skin, tea-like*. Mine said *thin*.

We were both right, which is the annoying thing about cupping. The coffee had
almost no body. Next to the other five it felt like someone had let a beautiful
aroma down with water. On the table, in sequence, after five bigger coffees, it
disappeared.

<Note title="What a cupping score is actually for">

An SCA score is a tool for comparing coffees under identical conditions, which
is exactly the condition a café never serves them in. A coffee that scores 88 on
a table can be a bad coffee for your bar if your grinder cannot hold it, your
water is soft, or your regulars all drink it with milk. We have bought 90-point
lots that nobody ordered twice.

</Note>

So we argued for two hours in a hotel lobby, which is not a metaphor.

## What changed my mind

Ambaye, the exporter, had been listening without saying much. Eventually he
suggested we cup the sixth lot on its own the following morning, first thing,
before anything else.

We did. Same coffee, same protocol, same water. Completely different coffee.

Without five heavier naturals immediately before it, the thing I had called thin
read as clean. The jasmine was not a top note, it was the whole structure. And
the finish — which I had not registered at all the day before — went on for
something like forty seconds.

I had scored it in the wrong context and written *thin* when I meant *not like
the one before it*.

## The part where we nearly lost it

By then two other buyers had been through the same table. One of them, a roaster
in Melbourne whose palate I would trust over mine on most days, had put an offer
on four of the six lots including ours.

We got it because Ambaye held it for twenty-four hours on a handshake, and
because we paid roughly double the C-market for it. That price is printed on the
bag. It is not a moral flourish — it is the number that made a farm agree to
sell to the smaller buyer.

<PullQuote cite="Rami, insufferably, for about a month afterwards">
  I had it at 88 on the Thursday.
</PullQuote>

## How to drink it

It is on the brew bar until the lot runs out, which at our volume is about nine
weeks. Order it as a V60, black, and give it four minutes to come down from
serving temperature — it does not properly open up until it is around 55°C,
which is the single most common reason people miss what a good natural is doing.

If you want it at home, the bag is on the retail shelf, stamped with the roast
date. Filter grind. Between seven and twenty-one days off roast is the window.

And if you taste it and think *thin* — come back the next morning and try it
first, before anything else. That is all it took for me.

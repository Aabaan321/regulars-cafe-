Joy Mendoza came third in the UAE Brewers Cup in February. Her competition
routine used a washed Ethiopian, a recipe she had rebuilt eleven times, and a
water profile she mixed herself from distilled water and two salts.

Ask her what the hardest drink on our menu is and she says karak. It costs
AED 18.

## "Anyone can make a good V60"

That is the first thing she said when I sat down with her, which is obviously
untrue and she knows it.

"No, I mean it. A V60 is one variable at a time. Grind, water, time, pour. If
it is bad you change one thing and you find out. Karak is eight things at once
and they all move."

She counted them off. Tea grade. Milk fat. Sugar timing. Cardamom, whole or
cracked. Saffron, which she adds at the end because it turns bitter if it boils.
The pot, which is aluminium and stained and which she will not let anyone
replace. The flame. And the eleven minutes.

"Eleven?"

"Between ten and twelve. You are boiling it, reducing it, boiling it again. If
you stop at six you have got sweet tea with milk in it. If you go to fifteen it
is syrup."

## The steamed version

Most karak in Dubai is not boiled any more. It is tea concentrate, steamed with
milk on an espresso machine steam wand, sweetened, out in forty seconds. It is
fine. It is what the volume demands when you are selling six hundred a day at a
cafeteria window.

Joy's objection is specific and not snobbery: "Steaming heats it. Boiling changes
it. The milk sugars caramelise, the tea tannins soften, the cardamom oils come
out into the fat. You cannot get there in forty seconds because it is not a
temperature thing, it is a time thing."

## Why it is still eighteen dirhams

We have had this argument internally more than once. A drink that occupies a
burner for eleven minutes and a person for four of them does not, on any
spreadsheet, belong at AED 18 in a café where a V60 is 34.

Nadia's position, which won: karak at a specialty price is a specialty café
telling the neighbourhood it is not for them.

So it stays at 18. It is made in batches of six through the morning and to order
after two. And it is, by a distance, the most-ordered drink we sell that is not
coffee.

## What she is working on now

A cold version. Not iced karak — which she describes, accurately, as "a
disappointment with ice in it" — but something built cold from the start, where
the cardamom is infused into the milk overnight and the tea is brewed as a
concentrate and cut at service.

It has been through nine versions. Four of them she poured away in front of me.

"It is close. It is not right. The problem is the fat — cold milk does not carry
cardamom the same way and you end up tasting it on the front of your tongue
instead of the back."

She will put it on when it is right, which she estimates is two months away, and
which I have learned means two months.

## Ask for it

Joy is on bar most weekday mornings and Saturday. If it is quiet, ask her to show
you the pot. She will talk for longer than you planned and you will leave knowing
more about tea than you expected to.
